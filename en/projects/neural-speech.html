The project

Neural Speech is the 3rd-year electronics project, carried out by a team of four. The goal: recognize spoken words entirely on an Arduino Due, from the microphone acquisition to the neural network classification. The PC is only used to prepare the data, train the model offline and display the result.

Our use case, PokECE, turns it into a Pokédex of the school's professors: the user says a professor's name, the board recognizes the word, lights up the matching LED and sends the result to a PC interface that displays the professor's “card”. Everything fits in a 3D-printed, Pokéball-shaped enclosure.

Processing chain

The sound picked up by the microphone goes through eight stages, all computed in real time on the Arduino Due:

  1. MicrophoneMAX9814 on input A0, biased at mid-supply
  2. 12-bit ADC≈ 32 kHz, triggered by timer TC0
  3. FIR filter31 taps, 3.4 kHz low-pass
  4. DecimationOne sample out of 4, i.e. ≈ 8 kHz
  5. Frames256 samples, 50 % overlap
  6. SpectrumPre-emphasis, Hamming window, 256-point FFT
  7. MFCC26 Mel filters, log, DCT: 13 coefficients
  8. CNNRecognizes the word, lights up the matching LED

Real-time filtering

The FIR filter works in fixed point (Q12 format) on a circular buffer: for each new sample, it computes a 31-product convolution, then decimation keeps only one sample out of four. Measured on the board, this processing takes 12 µs per sample, while the budget between two samples is 31 µs.

Embedded neural network

The network is trained on a PC with PyTorch, on a dataset of 314 recordings built by the team. Its weights are then exported to a self-contained C header, which also holds the standardization parameters and the inference code run by the Arduino: 

Input 62×13 MFCC
 → Conv 5×3, 8 filters, ReLU  → MaxPool 2×1   (31×13×8)
 → Conv 3×3, 12 filters, ReLU → MaxPool 2×1   (15×13×12)
 → Dense 2340 → 32, ReLU
 → Dense 32 → 3, softmax

Before inference, a level check (RMS and peak) discards captures that are too quiet: the system then answers “Unknown” rather than guessing.

Advanced features

  • Three words instead of two. The specification required recognizing at least two. With three words, the error even dropped from 3.3 % (2 out of 60) to 2.2 % (2 out of 90).
  • Voice-triggered recording. With the button, pressing and speaking had to be synchronized. The board now listens continuously: it compares the average signal level, over windows of about 10 ms, to a threshold that adapts to the background noise, and only triggers after several consecutive windows above the threshold. A 150 ms pre-roll buffer avoids cutting off the beginning of the word.

Hardware and enclosure

  • Arduino Due, required by the specification;
  • MAX9814 microphone module, with an automatic-gain preamplifier, on input A0;
  • three result LEDs protected by 220 Ω resistors;
  • a push button to start a manual acquisition;
  • a schematic drawn in KiCad;
  • a 3D-printed, Pokéball-shaped enclosure.
