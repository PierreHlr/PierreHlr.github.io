NFC business card

A business card that is also a printed circuit board: tap it with a phone and it opens this portfolio. No battery, no app, no microcontroller. The antenna is etched into the PCB and was designed by calculation, since there was no instrument to measure it.

Requirements

The goal: anyone should reach my portfolio just by holding their phone near the card. I set the requirements before drawing anything:

  • fully passive: no battery, no power supply, no microcontroller;
  • read by common NFC phones (iPhone XS and newer, Android), with no app;
  • read distance of at least 2 cm, 3 cm targeted;
  • content protected against rewriting;
  • a fallback QR code for phones without NFC;
  • the size of a bank card, to fit in a wallet.

Principle: a 13.56 MHz transformer

At 13.56 MHz the wavelength is 22 m, so a phone a few centimetres away is deep in the near field: nothing is really “radiated”. The phone's coil and the card's antenna behave like the primary and secondary of a loosely coupled air-core transformer.

  • Power: the phone's field induces a voltage in the antenna (Faraday's law). The chip rectifies it to power itself.
  • Answer: the chip modulates its own load. The phone senses this change through the mutual inductance, and decodes it.

At the minimum field allowed by ISO/IEC 14443 (1.5 A/m), the card's antenna only produces 2.0 V rms open-circuit. Resonance is what multiplies this voltage, so the whole project rests on sizing the antenna correctly.

Choosing the chip

The first candidate, the NXP NTAG213, is only sold as bare wafers or modules for card manufacturers: a PCB assembler cannot solder it. Its sibling, the NTAG213F, has the same memory (144 bytes) and the same input capacitance (50 pF), but comes in a solderable 2.0 × 1.5 mm HXSON4 package, stocked by JLCPCB.

The “F” adds a field-detect output (FD), meant to wake up a microcontroller. Without a microcontroller, the datasheet requires two precautions:

  • FD must not float: it is tied to the chip's ground. In return, the sleep mode (SLEEP_EN) must never be enabled during programming, or the card would become invisible;
  • the exposed pad stays unconnected: it is soldered for mechanical strength only.

Schematic

The schematic has three elements: the antenna, the chip, and two footprints for tuning capacitors, left empty at assembly.

Sizing the antenna

The antenna and the chip's input capacitance form a parallel LC circuit. The chip receives the highest voltage when this circuit resonates at the carrier frequency: 

f0 = 1 / (2π·√(L·Ctot))
Ctot = Cchip + Cparasitic + Ctuning

I aimed slightly above 13.56 MHz, between 14 and 15 MHz, for two reasons. A tuning capacitor can only add capacitance, so it can only lower the frequency: an antenna that resonates too high can be corrected, one that resonates too low means a new PCB. And the phone itself detunes the card, differently for each model: the best tuning is found by measurement. With the chip's 50 pF, this calls for an inductance of 2.1 to 2.6 µH.

The antenna runs around the card (80 × 48 mm), like the antenna of a contactless bank card, which every phone is designed to read. There is no copper area inside it: eddy currents in such an area would oppose the field.

Two independent calculations

Without a network analyser, the inductance could not be measured before ordering. To be able to trust the calculation, I used two methods that share no assumption, and only accepted a geometry when they agreed:

  • Greenhouse method: the spiral is cut into its 16 straight segments. The total inductance is the sum of every segment's self-inductance and of the mutual inductance of every pair of parallel segments, obtained by integrating the Neumann formula exactly. The code was validated on a 50 mm square loop: 210.6 nH, against 210.5 nH for the closed-form formula.
  • Empirical formula for printed antennas, from STMicroelectronics application note AN2972.

Both methods were then used to choose the number of turns and the trace width:

With 3 turns, the resonance is far too high. With 5 turns, or with 0.5 mm traces, it is already below the carrier, where no capacitor could bring it back up. 4 turns of 1.0 mm land in the target window with the widest traces, hence the lowest resistance. The two methods agree within 2.5 %: I kept L = 2.24 µH.

Trace width really matters: at 13.56 MHz the skin effect confines the current to about 18 µm, half the copper thickness. A first draft of the footprint had 0.2 mm traces: five times more resistance (2.3 Ω at DC) and a resonance already at 14.0 MHz, leaving almost no margin for tuning. With 1.0 mm, the resistance drops to 0.72 Ω at 13.56 MHz, for an unloaded quality factor of about 265.

Tuning without a network analyser

With L = 2.24 µH and 52.5 pF in total (50 pF for the chip, about 2.5 pF of parasitic capacitance between turns and on the pads), the card resonates at 14.7 MHz with no capacitor. About 9 pF more brings it down to 13.56 MHz.

A 5 % error on L or on the chip capacitance moves the untuned resonance between 14.3 and 15.1 MHz: in every case the card stays above the carrier and can be tuned down.

Without a network analyser, the resonance itself cannot be measured. What matters to the user, however, can be measured with a phone and a ruler: the maximum read distance. The card therefore has two 0603 footprints in parallel with the antenna, empty at assembly. Each of the five prototypes will get a different value, and the one that reads furthest across several phones will be kept:

Layout and manufacturing

A coil is a problem for KiCad: at DC it is a short circuit between its two ends, so a design rule check (DRC) error if it is drawn as tracks. The antenna is therefore a separate footprint, drawn with 16 copper lines and declared as a net tie: KiCad then knows the short is intentional.

  • the outer end of the spiral comes back inside through a bridge on the back layer, under the four turns;
  • the chip is rotated by 180° and the capacitors by 90°: every LB terminal ends up at the top, every LA terminal at the bottom, and the routing comes down to two parallel rails;
  • no ground plane and no copper pour: nothing must disturb the field inside the loop.

The cards are manufactured and assembled by JLCPCB: 0.8 mm FR-4, black solder mask, white silkscreen and ENIG (gold) finish, which also gives flat pads for the chip's 0.25 mm pins. Only the chip is placed by the assembler; the tuning capacitors will be soldered by hand.

Data and QR code

The phone opens the link through an NDEF URI record. The https:// prefix is not stored as text but as a single byte: the whole message fits in 28 bytes, 19 % of the chip's 144 bytes.

The fallback QR code holds the same address in upper case (HTTPS://PIERREHLR.GITHUB.IO/). Scheme and host names are case-insensitive, and upper case enables the alphanumeric mode: 11 bits per pair of characters instead of 16. The code then fits in 25 × 25 modules instead of 29 × 29; at 17.5 mm wide, each module measures 0.70 mm, well above the silkscreen resolution. It is printed as a knockout (white silkscreen for the background, black mask for the modules): otherwise the code would come out inverted, and many scanners would reject it.

Next steps

The five assembled cards are on order. The next steps:

  • measure the untuned read distance with several phones (iPhone and Android);
  • try the capacitor values on the five prototypes, and keep the one with the best worst-case distance;
  • write the URL with NXP TagWriter, check the message with NXP TagInfo, then write-lock the chip.

The measured distances will complete the tuning table above.
